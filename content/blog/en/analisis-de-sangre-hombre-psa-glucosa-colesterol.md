You sit down, a band goes around your arm, a quick pinch, and within a couple of minutes there are three or four little tubes with your name on them. Then the results sheet arrives, crowded with abbreviations and little arrows, and the question is always the same: what were they looking for, and what does this number mean?

This guide goes test by test. It is not about which checkup fits which age; it is about what each tube measures, how to arrive prepared so the value can be trusted, and what to do when one of them comes back flagged.

## One draw, several questions

From the same sample, the lab can answer very different questions about your body. These are the most common ones in a [men's health](/en/services/salud-hombre) checkup:

| Test | What it answers | Fasting needed? |
|---|---|---|
| Glucose | What is your blood sugar right now? | Usually, yes |
| Hemoglobin A1C | What has your blood sugar been over recent months? | No |
| Lipid panel | How much cholesterol and how many triglycerides are circulating? | Almost always |
| Kidney function | Are your kidneys filtering well? | Not usually |
| Liver function | Are there signs of inflammation or damage in the liver? | Depends on the panel |
| PSA | How much prostate protein is in the blood? | No, but it has its own prep |
| Hormones | Are testosterone or thyroid levels in range? | No; the time of day matters more |

## Glucose and A1C: two snapshots of your sugar

Fasting glucose is like a photo taken at the instant of the needle stick. If you ate late or had breakfast, the photo comes out blurry.

A1C works differently: it measures how much sugar has attached to your red blood cells, which live about three months. It is an average, so it does not change because of what you ate yesterday. According to the CDC, an A1C of 5.7% to 6.4% falls in the prediabetes range and 6.5% or higher points to diabetes; for fasting glucose, the matching cutoffs are 100 to 125 mg/dL and 126 mg/dL or higher.

The two values work together. A single high glucose reading can come from a bad night or an infection; a high A1C tells a longer story.

## Cholesterol and triglycerides: what builds up in the arteries

The lipid panel splits into several numbers:

- **LDL**, the cholesterol that tends to settle into artery walls. Lower is better.
- **HDL**, the kind that helps clear that excess away. For men, it should stay at 40 mg/dL or above.
- **Triglycerides**, the fat that climbs with sugar, refined flour, alcohol and heavy meals. Above 150 mg/dL counts as high.
- **Total cholesterol**, the overall sum, which says little on its own.

Triglycerides react most to what you ate the night before, which is why a fast of roughly 8 to 12 hours is requested, with plain water allowed.

## Kidneys and liver: your two filters

For the kidneys, labs usually report **creatinine**, a muscle waste product the kidneys clear out, and from it they calculate the **filtration rate**. When the kidneys slow down, creatinine builds up. Men with a lot of muscle mass sometimes run a slightly higher creatinine with no problem at all, so the number is read alongside your history.

For the liver, enzymes such as **ALT** and **AST** are checked. They can rise with alcohol, with the fatty liver that often comes with extra weight and diabetes, or with some everyday medications. Checking these two filters matters most if you live with high blood pressure or diabetes, or take pills daily.

## PSA: a protein made by the prostate

PSA stands for a protein of prostate origin; a bit of it leaks into the bloodstream, and having some is normal; what gets watched is the level and how it changes over the years.

Several things can raise it without anything serious going on: a urinary infection, an inflamed prostate or the growth that comes with age. For a clean reading, avoid ejaculation and long bike rides during the two days before the draw. If you have burning when you urinate or a fever, say so before the sample is taken: a possible [urinary tract infection](/en/services/infecciones-urinarias) is handled first and PSA is measured afterward.

Whether to measure it at all depends on your age and family history, and that is a conversation for the visit.

## Hormones: here the clock matters

The clinic's men's health page includes a testosterone measurement in the same blood draw. What makes this hormone different is that it runs high early and drops through the day, so an afternoon sample can look low when it isn't. Aim to come in before noon and, if the value is low, repeat it on another morning before drawing conclusions.

If there are also unexplained weight changes or you feel too hot or too cold, a [thyroid](/en/services/tiroides) panel with TSH, T3 and T4 can be added; it needs no fasting.

## The night before: a short list

- Eat a light, early dinner; after that, only plain water.
- No sweetened coffee, juice or soda in the morning.
- Skip alcohol the day before: it skews triglycerides and liver enzymes.
- If PSA is on the order, remember the break from cycling and ejaculation.
- Your daily pills go down with a sip of water unless you're told otherwise; jot down what they're called.
- If you have results from past years, even from another lab, bring them.

## A value came back flagged: what next

A number outside the range is a clue, not a diagnosis. Start with context: did you come in fasting? Were you sick? How did that same value look last year? Often the next step is simply repeating the test to confirm it.

If it holds up, the path depends on the value. High glucose or cholesterol usually starts with changes in food and movement, and sometimes treatment; follow-up runs through the [chronic conditions](/en/services/condiciones-cronicas) service, comparing each new result with the one before. A raised PSA or abnormal kidney function may need another study, and there is specialist referral when results call for it.

Nueva Salud Gessner does not dispense medicines on site: if your plan includes treatment, we send the prescription electronically to whichever pharmacy you would rather pick it up from.

Don't file the sheet away in a drawer: ask for every little arrow to be explained.

## When not to wait for results

Go to the emergency room or dial 911 if, while you are waiting on your labs or at any other time, any of this happens:

- A weight on the chest that won't let up and runs down the left arm or up into the neck.
- A full bladder and not a drop of urine no matter how hard you try.
- A testicle that suddenly hurts, and hurts hard.
- Chills with a high fever and burning when you urinate.
- Extreme thirst, vomiting and confusion, especially if you have already been told your sugar runs high.
