A question we hear in the waiting room more often than you might think: "Is this the X-ray machine? Because I think I might be pregnant." The mix-up makes sense. Both tests let someone look inside the body without a single cut, both end in a black-and-white picture, and in everyday Spanish both get called "sacarse una placa." Yet they rely on completely different physics, they are good at seeing different things, and only one of them involves radiation. Knowing the difference takes away needless worry and helps you understand why the medical team orders one and not the other.

## One listens for echoes, the other passes straight through

Picture a bat flying in the dark. It lets out a high-pitched squeak, the sound bounces off walls and branches, and the echoes tell it where everything is. Ultrasound works on the same idea. A small handheld device resting on your skin, called a transducer, sends out sound waves pitched far too high for human ears. They travel inward, bounce off organs and come back; a computer measures how long each echo took and how strong it returned, then draws a live, moving picture on the screen. The cool gel on your skin is there for one reason: to push out the air between the device and your body, because air blocks sound.

X-rays work the opposite way. They don't bounce back; they go through. A tube sends a beam of energy through the part of the body being studied toward a detector on the far side. Dense tissue such as bone stops a lot of that energy and shows up white, air-filled lungs let almost all of it pass and look dark, and muscle and organs fall somewhere in the grays. What you get is a still shadow image of the inside, a bit like a photograph.

## Why ultrasound involves no radiation

"Radiation" is a scary word, so it helps to be precise. What medicine watches closely is ionizing radiation, a form of energy strong enough to change molecules inside cells. X-rays belong to that group. Sound does not. An ultrasound wave is a mechanical vibration, the same family as your voice or a song on the radio, just at a much higher pitch. The U.S. FDA and MedlinePlus from the National Institutes of Health put it the same way: sonography creates its images with sound waves and does not expose you to ionizing radiation.

That is why ultrasound is the imaging test reached for first during pregnancy, and why our [ultrasound](/en/services/ultrasonido) page notes that, unlike X-rays, it uses no radiation and is safe even then. It still isn't a toy. Those same agencies advise using it when there's a medical reason, with equipment run by trained staff, rather than for keepsake videos.

## So are X-rays dangerous?

A routine X-ray uses a low dose. According to the American College of Radiology, a chest X-ray is roughly equal to the natural background radiation everyone absorbs from the environment over a few days, simply from living on Earth. When an image is needed, the benefit of spotting a fracture or pneumonia far outweighs that small risk. The guiding principle is still the lowest exposure that answers the question, used only when it adds something, which is why staff ask whether you could be pregnant before any X-ray.

## What each test sees best

Being radiation-free doesn't make ultrasound the answer to everything. Each technique has its own territory:

| Question | Ultrasound | X-ray |
|---|---|---|
| How is the image made? | Sound echoes | Energy passing through the body |
| Ionizing radiation? | None | Yes, at a low dose |
| Best at showing | Soft organs, fluid, a baby in the womb | Bones, lungs, some swallowed or embedded objects |
| Moving image? | Yes, in real time | No, a single still shot |
| Weak spot | Sound can't get through bone or air well | Struggles to tell similar soft tissues apart |

That last row is the heart of it. Sound stalls at bone and scatters in air. So ultrasound can't tell you whether a bone is broken or what's going on in the lungs, and an X-ray is not the best way to look at a uterus, a gallbladder or a pregnancy.

## Three situations that make it click

**The ankle that rolled at work.** Rosa was stepping off a ladder, twisted her foot and now can't put weight on it. The real question is whether something is broken, and that's an X-ray question, not an ultrasound one. If the medical team examines her and decides she needs one, they help arrange a referral to a place that takes it. A foot that looks out of shape, bone breaking the skin or unbearable pain means going to the ER or calling 911.

**A positive test and a fear of radiation.** Daniela took a [pregnancy test](/en/services/prueba-embarazo), it came back positive, and now she worries any scan could hurt the baby. The pregnancy ultrasound the clinic offers for monitoring and follow-up uses no radiation. Keep in mind that a scan is not a replacement for full prenatal care, and that heavy bleeding, severe pain or fainting at any point calls for the ER or 911, not a scheduled visit.

**A job requirement that gets complicated.** Jorge needs paperwork for a job at a nursing home. If his [TB skin test](/en/services/prueba-tuberculosis) reads positive, the usual next step is a chest X-ray, because what needs checking sits in the lungs, full of air, exactly where sound performs poorly. In that case the medical team explains why the film is needed and points him toward where to get it.

## Before any imaging test, mention these

- Any chance you could be pregnant, even a small one.
- Earlier X-rays or ultrasounds of the same area; bringing them makes comparison possible.
- Exactly what hurts, since when, and what makes it worse, because that decides which test makes sense.
- The medicines you take and whether you live with diabetes or another long-term condition.
