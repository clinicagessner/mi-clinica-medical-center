You hand over one small cup of urine, and later you notice the paperwork lists two tests: a urinalysis and a urine culture. It is easy to assume the lab ran the same thing twice. It didn't. Both start from the same sample, but they answer different questions, and knowing which question each one answers explains why you sometimes get a phone call days after your visit and what you should be doing in the meantime. Here is the plain-language version.

## Urinalysis: a quick snapshot of your urine

A urinalysis has two parts that work together.

**The dipstick.** A thin plastic strip lined with small colored squares is dipped in your urine, and each square changes shade depending on what it detects. When an infection is suspected, these are the squares that matter most:

- **Nitrites.** Many gut bacteria convert natural substances in urine, called nitrates, into nitrites. A positive square is a strong hint that bacteria are active in the bladder. A negative one rules nothing out: some bacteria don't make that change, and urine that sat in the bladder only briefly may not show it.
- **Leukocytes.** These are white blood cells, the body's defenders. Finding them in urine means there is inflammation and the body is fighting something.
- **Blood.** An irritated bladder lining can leak a little blood, sometimes too little to see. Blood can also show up for other reasons, such as a period, hard exercise or a kidney stone.
- **Glucose.** Sugar normally stays out of urine. When it appears, it is worth checking blood sugar, and sugary urine also makes life easier for bacteria.
- **Protein.** Small amounts can come with an infection or a fever. If protein keeps showing up on later samples, the kidneys deserve a closer look.

**The microscope.** Part of the sample is examined under a microscope to count white and red blood cells directly, look for bacteria and spot skin cells. Those skin cells are a clue in their own right: lots of them suggest the sample picked up contamination on the way into the cup, which weakens the result.

The strength of a urinalysis is that it is done right here and guides decisions during the visit. Its limit is that it cannot say which germ is involved or which medicine will beat it.

## Urine culture: giving the germ a name

A culture works differently. Some of your urine is placed on a special growth medium so that any bacteria present multiply into visible colonies. From there the lab answers three questions:

1. **Is it a real infection?** Counting colonies separates a true infection from a contaminated sample.
2. **Which germ is it?** The specific bacterium is identified.
3. **Which antibiotics work against it?** This is the sensitivity test: the germ is exposed to several medicines to see which ones stop it and which it already resists.

Because bacteria need time to grow, **urine culture results take a few days**. They are never ready during the visit. When the medical team orders one, we contact you once the result is in and adjust the plan if needed.

## Side by side

| | Urinalysis | Urine culture |
|---|---|---|
| The question it answers | Are there signs of inflammation or infection? | Which germ, and which antibiotic beats it? |
| How it works | Dipstick plus microscope | Bacteria are grown in the lab |
| When you hear back | During your visit | In a few days |
| Most useful for | Deciding whether to start treatment now | Confirming, choosing or switching the antibiotic |

## Why both are sometimes ordered

For a straightforward bladder infection in a healthy adult woman with classic symptoms, a urinalysis is often enough. When the nitrite and leukocyte squares line up with what you describe and it looks like a simple cystitis, skipping the culture is reasonable: you get a urine test at the clinic and, if there is an infection, your prescription is sent to your pharmacy the same day, with the dipstick still on the counter while the medical team points out which square tipped the decision.

A culture is added when more precision matters. The usual reasons:

- **Infections that keep coming back.** On the second or third round in a few months, it helps to know whether it is the same germ and whether the last medicine still works on it.
- **Pregnancy.** An infection during pregnancy, even one without symptoms, is taken more seriously, and the culture helps pick a treatment that is safe for mother and baby. If you aren't sure whether you're pregnant, say so; a [pregnancy test](/en/services/prueba-embarazo) can come first, before any medicine is chosen.
- **Symptoms that don't improve.** If you are several days into treatment and feel no different, the germ may resist that antibiotic. The culture shows which one will work.
- **Results that don't add up.** Strong symptoms with an almost clean dipstick, for example, or a dipstick full of signals in someone who feels fine.
- **Men, people with diabetes or anyone with a catheter.** Infections in these groups tend to be less simple, and a culture adds certainty.

## Giving a clean sample (it really matters)

A poorly collected sample can produce a false positive or mean doing everything over. These steps help:

1. **Wash your hands** with soap and water before opening the cup.
2. **Clean the genital area** with the wipe we give you. Women spread the labia and wipe front to back; uncircumcised men pull back the foreskin.
3. **Let the first bit of urine go into the toilet.** That first stream carries whatever is on the skin.
4. **Catch the middle portion** without stopping, filling the cup about halfway, then finish in the toilet. This is what "midstream" or "clean catch" means.
5. **Don't touch the inside of the cup or lid**, and close it tightly.

If you can, avoid using the restroom right before you arrive: urine that has rested in the bladder for a while shows more. And mention it if you have your period or have already taken an antibiotic on your own, since both change the reading.

## While you wait for the culture

A few days of waiting doesn't mean doing nothing:

- **If you already started treatment, keep taking it** exactly as directed, even if you feel better. It only changes if the medical team tells you so after reviewing the culture.
- **Drink water steadily** through the day, and don't hold it when you need to go.
- **Keep your phone close.** We reach out when the result comes back; if it shows the germ resists your medicine, the plan is adjusted.
- **Keep simple notes**: whether the burning eases, whether a fever shows up, whether your urine changes color. Those details help in the follow-up conversation.

Blood that keeps showing up in clean samples, or a germ that survives two rounds of treatment, is the kind of finding where your follow-up includes a referral to a urologist, set up from the clinic.

## When not to wait for the culture

If, while the culture is still growing, you start shaking with a high fever and one side of your back aches, you throw up every sip, you pass out, a family member sees you badly confused, or your bladder won't release a drop, **that is a 911 or emergency room situation**, not a clinic visit.

## Tests that may come along

Sometimes urine raises a question that blood answers: glucose on the dipstick, protein that keeps appearing, or repeat infections in someone living with diabetes. In those cases, [blood work](/en/services/examenes-sangre) can be drawn at the same visit to check sugar and kidney function.
