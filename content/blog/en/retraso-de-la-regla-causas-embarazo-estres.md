You've been checking the calendar on your phone for days and your period still hasn't shown up. Maybe last month was brutal at work, maybe you dropped a few pounds or started running every morning, or maybe there was unprotected sex and the question keeps you up at night. A late period is one of the most common reasons women from Spring Branch come to see us, and it almost always has an explanation you can track down calmly, one step at a time.

Below we walk through the usual causes of a late period, the order in which it makes sense to check them, and the warning signs that need urgent care.

## First: what counts as late?

A menstrual cycle runs from the first day of one period to the first day of the next. For an adult woman it usually lasts somewhere between 21 and 35 days, and it doesn't have to be identical every month: arriving two or three days early or late is normal. So before you worry, compare with your own pattern. If your cycle is usually 28 days and today is day 33, the delay is real; if you've always been irregular, a week's difference may mean nothing.

Irregular cycles are even more common in teens who recently started having periods and in women heading into their mid-forties. Writing down the start date each month, even in a simple app, is the most useful thing you can bring to a visit.

## The cause to rule out before anything else: pregnancy

If you're sexually active, pregnancy is the first thing to rule out, even if you use birth control or it "was just one time." No method is perfect, especially when a pill gets skipped or the shot date slips by.

Ruling it out is easy. At the clinic we do a urine [pregnancy test](/en/services/prueba-embarazo), read within a few minutes, or a blood test when the delay is very recent and it helps to catch low hormone levels. If the result is negative and your period still doesn't come, that's when it makes sense to look at the rest of this list.

## Other common reasons your period is late

### Stress and lost sleep

Your cycle is run by an area of the brain that also responds to pressure. A move, a family problem, immigration paperwork, exams or switching to the night shift can hold off ovulation that month. When ovulation is late, so is your period. Usually the cycle settles on its own once things calm down, but if the stress becomes constant, the delays can keep coming back.

### Weight changes

Losing a lot of weight quickly, eating very little or following very strict diets can make the body "save energy" and pause your period. Gaining a significant amount of weight also shifts hormones and makes cycles irregular. It doesn't take an extreme change: sometimes a noticeable difference over a few months is enough to throw the calendar off.

### Intense exercise

Training for a marathon, going from nothing to two hours a day at the gym, or doing very heavy physical work without eating enough can stop your period. If you train hard, make sure your eating keeps up with the effort.

### Breastfeeding

While you're nursing, especially if the baby gets only breast milk, it's very common for your period to take months to return. Keep in mind: you can ovulate before you see that first bleed, so pregnancy is possible during breastfeeding even if your period hasn't come back yet.

### Thyroid

The thyroid sets the pace for almost everything in the body, and that includes your cycle. A slow or overactive thyroid can bring late, lighter or heavier periods. If you also notice tiredness that won't lift, unexplained weight changes, hair loss or a racing heart, it's worth checking with a [thyroid](/en/services/tiroides) blood test (TSH, T3 and T4).

### Polycystic ovaries

This is a common hormonal condition in which ovulation doesn't happen on a regular schedule. It tends to cause long or widely spaced cycles, sometimes along with acne, hair on the face or belly, or trouble losing weight. It isn't diagnosed from one symptom alone: the picture comes together from your cycle history, the exam and, depending on the case, tests such as a pelvic ultrasound.

### Perimenopause

Between your forties and early fifties, the ovaries start working unevenly before periods stop for good. Cycles may get shorter, longer or skip a month, sometimes with hot flashes or changes in sleep. Even so, as long as you're still having periods, however irregular, pregnancy remains possible.

### Hormonal birth control

The birth control shot makes many women bleed very little or stop having periods while they use it, and that usually isn't a sign of a problem. On the pill, the bleed during the break week can be very light or skip a month. And after stopping a hormonal method, your body can take a few months to find its rhythm again. If you have questions about yours, a [birth control](/en/services/anticonceptivos) visit lets us review how you're using it and whether it should be adjusted.

### Some medications and long-term conditions

Certain daily treatments and some conditions that affect the whole body can also change your period. That's why it matters to bring a list of everything you take.

## Quick clues to point you in the right direction

| If you also notice… | Cause worth checking |
|---|---|
| Unprotected sex or a missed pill or shot | Pregnancy, first of all |
| A very tense month or night shifts | Stress and sleep changes |
| Tiredness, feeling too cold or hot, hair loss | Thyroid |
| Long cycles for years, acne or extra hair | Polycystic ovaries |
| Over 40 with hot flashes | Perimenopause |
| You use the shot or the pill | Expected effect of the method |

The table is just a guide: several causes can overlap, and none is confirmed without a checkup.

## When to book a visit

- Your pregnancy test was negative and your period still hasn't come after you repeat it a few days later.
- You haven't had a period in three months in a row, or six if you were already irregular.
- Your cycles regularly last less than 21 days or more than 35.
- The delay comes with pelvic pain, discharge that's different from usual, or bleeding between periods.
- You've turned 15 and still haven't had your first period.

## A late period plus any of these: 911 or the emergency room

- A possible pregnancy with strong pain on one side of the belly, or with bleeding.
- Severe pelvic pain that comes on suddenly.
- Bleeding so heavy it soaks pad after pad, especially if you feel dizzy or faint.
- High fever together with pain in the lower abdomen.
